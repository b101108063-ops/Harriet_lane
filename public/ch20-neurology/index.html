I. NEUROLOGIC EXAMINATION#

A. Mental Status#

  • Infants: Observe “cuteness” and ability to dynamically engage caretakers
  • Toddlers: Bring toys; observe and engage in play
  • School age: Ask children to draw or describe school or friends

C. Motor#

1. Muscle bulk: Atrophy or pseudohypertrophy is a red flag.#

2. Tone: Spasticity, rigidity, hypotonia#

4. Red flags: Scissoring, toe-walking, inability to supinate hand, clasped thumb#

II. NEUROLOGIC EMERGENCIES#

A. Stroke#

1. Presentation and etiology:#

a. 50% ischemic, 50% hemorrhagic overall in pediatric stroke

b. Approximately 80% ischemic in perinatal stroke

*c. Stroke mimics are more common than stroke itself (migraine, seizure,

functional disorders, mass lesions)

2. Etiologies vary by age:#

Perinatal AIS Childhood AIS Cerebral Venous Thrombosis
Not fully understood Cardiac disease, cerebral arteriopathy Inherited thrombophilia
Combination of maternal and fetal factors Hypercoagulable states Drugs, infections
Sickle cell disease Dehydration, nephrotic syndrome

3. Management:#

a. Stroke team activation or urgent neurology consultation b. Supportive care: Maintain normoglycemia, normothermia, oxygenation *c. Reperfusion therapies: IV tPA or mechanical thrombectomy may be

considered in appropriate circumstances

*d. Children with sickle cell disease: Hydration and emergent exchange

transfusion

B. Status Epilepticus and Breakthrough Seizures#

1. Definition: Continuous seizure activity ≥5 minutes or two or more#

discrete seizures without return of consciousness between them.

2. Breakthrough seizures: Assess for missed medications, lack of sleep,#

stress, drugs/alcohol, illness, dehydration, drug interactions.

C. Encephalopathy#

1. Definitions:#

*a. Encephalopathy: Diffuse neuronal dysfunction manifesting as acute

or chronic altered mental status

*b. Encephalitis: Inflammation of brain parenchyma secondary to

infection or inflammatory response

2. Selected causes of encephalopathy:#

  • Infectious: meningitis, encephalitis, ADEM
  • Autoimmune: NMDAR, VGKC-complex, Hashimoto’s
  • Trauma, toxins, metabolic disorders
  • Hypertension, hypoxic-ischemic injury

D. Hydrocephalus with Increased Intracranial Pressure#

1. Clinical signs: Macrocephaly, increasing head circumference, bulging#

fontanelle, headaches, vomiting, Cushing’s triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations), papilledema

2. Treatment:#

a. Medical: Acetazolamide and furosemide may decrease CSF production b. Surgical: CSF shunting vs. endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV)

E. Weakness#

1. Botulism#

a. Etiology: Clostridium botulinum toxin irreversibly cleaves protein

complex necessary for acetylcholine vesicle release

*b. Clinical features: Subacute onset weakness concentrating in eye, face,

and bulbar muscles early. Dilated and poorly reactive pupils.

c. Treatment: Human botulism immune globulin (BabyBIG); Airway

management; ~25% require intubation

2. Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)#

*a. Etiology: Immune attack against peripheral nerve myelin;

triggered by Campylobacter jejuni, Zika, SARS-CoV-2

*b. Clinical features: Ascending weakness and sensory loss with rapid

decline; ~20% require mechanical ventilation

*c. Diagnosis: Elevated spinal fluid protein without cellular infiltrate

(“albuminocytologic dissociation”) in 50–70%

d. Treatment: IVIG (0.4 g/kg daily for 5 days) or plasmapheresis

F. Ataxia#

1. Definition: Impaired coordination of movement and balance; broad-based gait#

2. Differential diagnosis of acute ataxia:#

  • Ingestion (antiseizure meds, alcohol, heavy metals)
  • Postinfectious: acute cerebellar ataxia, cerebellitis
  • Head trauma: cerebellar contusion or hemorrhage
  • Basilar migraine, benign paroxysmal vertigo
  • Opsoclonus myoclonus ataxia syndrome: Consider neuroblastoma
  • GBS or Miller-Fisher variant

III. CHRONIC OR GRADUALLY PROGRESSIVE CONDITIONS#

A. Headaches#

1. Classification:#

a. Primary headaches: Migraine, tension type, cluster, TACs *b. Secondary headaches: Trauma, infection, vascular disorder,

increased intracranial pressure

2. Migraine headache:#

a. Can be throbbing, pulsatile, or pressure-like b. Usually bifrontal in children, unilateral in adolescents/adults *c. Many potential triggers (stress, caffeine, menses, sleep disruption)

3. Treatment:#

*a. Acute: NSAIDs (naproxen, ibuprofen), acetaminophen;

Triptans at migraine onset; Avoid medication overuse

b. Preventive: Propranolol, Amitriptyline, Topiramate

Lifestyle modification: adequate sleep, meals, hydration, regular exercise, weight loss if overweight

B. Seizures#

1. Definition: Paroxysmal, transient, synchronized discharge of cortical#

neurons resulting in alteration of function

2. Febrile seizures:#

*a. Simple febrile seizure: Primary generalized, 6–60 months, nonfocal,

<15 minutes, does not recur in 24 hours (1) Management: Identify source of fever; No neuroimaging, EEG, or

bloodwork needed for simple febrile seizure in well-appearing, fully immunized child

b. Complex febrile seizure: Focal, >15 minutes, or recurs within 24 hours

3. Treatment of unprovoked seizure:#

*a. First seizure, nonfocal, return to baseline: Usually no antiseizure

medication indicated; ~50% recurrence in 2–5 years

*b. Pharmacotherapy: Choose according to seizure type (see Table 20.2)

4. Selected epilepsy syndromes:#

Syndrome Evaluation Treatment Comments
Infantile spasms EEG with hypsarrhythmia; MRI High-dose steroids or ACTH; Vigabatrin Onset 2+ months; peak 4–6 months
Childhood absence seizures EEG with 3–4 Hz spike-wave Ethosuximide, lamotrigine, valproic acid Hyperventilation precipitates seizure

C. Neuromuscular Disorders#

1. Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)#

a. Etiology: Autosomal recessive mutations in SMN1 gene; Severity

correlates with SMN2 copy number

*b. Treatment: Nusinersen (Spinraza), Onasemnogene abeparvovec

(Zolgensma), Risdiplam (Evrysdi)

2. Duchenne/Becker muscular dystrophies (DMD/BMD)#

a. Etiology: X-linked mutation in DMD gene encoding dystrophin *b. Treatment: Corticosteroids (prednisone, deflazacort); Exon-skipping

therapies (eteplirsen, viltolarsen, golodirsen, casimersen)

3. Myasthenia gravis#

a. Etiology: Autoantibodies binding AChR or MuSK *b. Clinical features: Fatigable weakness; Orbital muscles (ptosis,

diplopia); Bulbar weakness (slurred/nasal voice, difficulty chewing, swallowing)

c. Treatment:

(1) Myasthenic crisis: Plasmapheresis, IVIG, IV neostigmine **(2) Chronic: Pyridostigmine, prednisone,

immunosuppressants (azathioprine, mycophenolate, rituximab)

D. Functional Neurologic Disorders (FNDs)#

1. Definition: Symptoms of altered voluntary motor or sensory function#

not explained by recognized neurologic or medical conditions

2. Diagnosis:#

a. Hoover’s sign: Weakness of hip extension returns to normal with

contralateral hip flexion against resistance

b. Breakaway/give-way weakness c. Variability in frequency of tremor

3. Management:#

a. Physical therapy rehabilitation and cognitive behavioral therapy
